— 00

What We're Trying to Learn

The white paper sets a clear direction. But a vision document is only as good as the specific architecture that follows it — and that architecture can only be as good as our understanding of CivilGrid's actual operations. Three open questions shape everything else. This engagement exists to answer them.

01

Where is the honest signal?

The intelligence layer is only as good as the signal feeding it. Is CivilGrid's behavioral data consolidated, queryable, and machine-readable — or fragmented across disconnected tools that no AI can read continuously?

Via

Tool inventory + signal audit workshop + analytics access

02

What is the minimum viable world model?

Too thin and the AI can't surface useful context. Too thick and the team won't maintain it — it becomes the wiki nobody reads. The right answer lives in observing what information people actually seek out day to day.

Via

Documentation audit + IC interviews + meeting observation

03

Will the team run toward this or away?

An architecturally perfect model that nobody adopts is a failure. Cultural readiness shapes the rollout sequence — and identifying the right first DRI is as important as any technical decision.

Via

Team interviews + org mapping + leadership sessions

— 02

Week 1 — Orient & Map the Current State

Don't interpret yet. The job this week is to understand the landscape without projecting onto it: what tools exist, where information lives, what the signals are and who has access to them. Every assumption we arrive with should be challenged before Day 6.

Goal
Complete map of the information environment
By end of week: we know what every team uses, where their knowledge lives, what signals the product generates, and which of those signals are machine-readable today.
DAYS 1–2

Kickoff + Tool Inventory

Kickoff session with CTO and Head of Product: what do they most want to change, and what do they most want to preserve. Then a full tool inventory — every SaaS product the company pays for, what it's used for, who owns it, and whether it has an API or export capability. Request access to all relevant systems before Day 3.

Key question: Where does institutional knowledge live today — Notion? Confluence? Google Docs? Slack threads? Someone's notebook?

→ tool-inventory.md
DAYS 3–4

Leadership Interviews

Five interviews, 45 minutes each. One open question per conversation: "Walk me through the last time a decision in your area was slower than it should have been. What information were you waiting for, and where was it?"

Interview Focus
CEO Strategic context, what "throughput" means specifically, what they're afraid of losing
CTO Engineering cycle time, where decisions stack up, current documentation practice
Head of Product How signal reaches roadmap decisions — and what never does
Head of Data How coverage decisions are made, how demand signals are tracked today
Head of CS What customer feedback never reaches product, patterns nobody else is seeing
→ leadership-interviews.md
DAY 5

Signal Audit Workshop

Two-hour working session with Head of Product, Head of Data, and one senior engineer. Goal: map every meaningful signal the business generates and trace it to where it lives, who has access, and how stale it gets. This is the single most important event of Week 1.

The workshop produces a draft signal map in this form — blank cells are the problem we are solving:

Signal Source Tool Accessible to Lag
Layer activation patterns Product ? ? ?
Coming Soon click volume Product ? ? ?
Project location data Product ? ? ?
Session return depth Product ? ? ?
Support ticket patterns CS / Support ? ? ?
Account health signals CS ? ? ?
Dataset coverage requests Data ? ? ?
Sales pipeline velocity Sales ? ? ?

Decision gate: If more than half the signal is siloed in tools with no API or export, Phase 1 of the implementation plan becomes a data consolidation sprint before anything else.

→ signal-map.md
— 03

Week 2 — Deep Dives by Function

Go deep in each function: Engineering, Product, Data, Customer Success. Measure what can be measured. Trace how information actually flows — not how people think it flows. The gap between those two things is where the architecture earns its keep.

Goal
Baseline metrics + signal flow map for each function
By end of week: we know current cycle time, coordination overhead, and signal flow for engineering, product, data, and CS. We have identified the five highest-cost coordination patterns in the company.
DAYS 6–8

Engineering

Interview three engineers at IC level. Review the last ten engineering decisions — where was the decision recorded? how long from "question raised" to "decision made"? Audit all existing documentation: what exists, where, how current.

  • → What is the current average time from ticket created to ticket shipped?
  • → What percentage of time goes to coordination vs. building?
  • → What is the most expensive recurring coordination pattern?
  • → Does the team write ADRs, RFCs, or anything that captures reasoning — not just decisions?
  • → What context do engineers wish they had before starting a ticket?
→ engineering-baseline.md
DAYS 8–9

Product

Review the current roadmap: format, location, who maintains it, how often it changes. Trace one recent feature from "idea" to "spec" — how many meetings, how many people, how long. Map the full path from signal to decision.

  • → How does a Coming Soon click currently reach a roadmap prioritization meeting? How many hops? How much lag?
  • → What product decisions in the last six months were made on gut rather than data, and why?
  • → What is the PM function's biggest time sink today?
→ product-signal-flow.md
DAY 9

Data Team

Understand the coverage matrix: how it's maintained, where it lives, who can edit it, how engineering learns about changes. Assess whether Coming Soon clicks are being systematically reviewed — and by whom.

  • → When a new state goes live, what is the notification chain? How does CS find out? Engineering?
  • → What data does the team produce that nobody seems to use?
  • → What questions does the rest of the company ask repeatedly that the data team has to manually answer?
→ data-team-flows.md
DAYS 10–11

Customer Success

Read the last thirty CS account notes — look for patterns and recurring requests that haven't reached the product team. Interview two CSMs. Observe one customer call if possible.

  • → What customer feedback do you give repeatedly that never seems to reach the roadmap?
  • → Which accounts are most at risk right now, and does engineering know why?
  • → How do you communicate "this customer needs X dataset in Y state" to the data team?
→ cs-signal-gaps.md
— 04

Week 3 — Shadowing & Team Interviews

The most important week. What people tell you in an interview and what actually happens in a meeting are often two different things. This week we observe without participating, interview without leading, and map the human network that the org chart doesn't show.

Goal
Org map with named DRI candidates and change readiness assessment
By end of week: we know who the informal knowledge holders are, who is excited about this model and who is skeptical, and who the first DRI should be — by name.

Meetings to observe — without participating

Meeting What to watch for
Engineering standup / planning How is context provided? How long does it take engineers to understand what they're building and why? How many questions could have been answered by a document?
Product–Engineering sync What information is being relayed that should already be written down? What decisions are made in real-time that could have been async?
CS–Product feedback loop How much signal is compressed, distorted, or lost in the relay? What does CS know that Product visibly doesn't?
Any all-hands or company sync What does leadership communicate that would be better as a substrate file, maintained continuously instead of repeated weekly?

The recurring information gap that someone mentions in an interview becomes a substrate file. The repeated question to the same person becomes an intelligence layer query. Look for the patterns, not the anecdotes.

IC Interviews — Days 12–15

Six to eight interviews across Engineering, Data, and CS. One open question: "Tell me about a time you needed information to do your job and it took longer than it should have to get it."

What to listen for:

  • → Recurring information gaps — these become substrate files
  • → Repeated questions sent to the same person — these become intelligence layer queries
  • → Decisions made without full context — these reveal where the world model is thin
  • → The informal coordination network — who does everyone go to? These are DRI and player-coach candidates

Org Structure Mapping — Days 14–15

Full org chart with reporting lines, tenure, and self-described expertise. Then the harder map: who has the most institutional knowledge? Who is currently doing informal coordination work that should be systematized? Who is most likely to thrive as a DRI, and who needs a player-coach rather than a manager title?

Assess change readiness honestly — not just who is excited, but whose resistance would stall the rollout. An architecture that lands with leadership but fails with two senior engineers is still a failed architecture.

→ org-map.md
— 05

Week 4 — Synthesize & Deliver

Turn everything gathered into a specific, validated architecture. Draft, pressure-test with the people who will implement it, revise, and deliver.

Goal
A revised architecture document CivilGrid can act on immediately
By Day 30: a specific substrate taxonomy with named owners, a signal consolidation roadmap, a role model with named pilot candidates, and a 24-week implementation plan with milestones.
DAYS 16–18

Draft the Architecture

Produce the first version of the implementation plan using everything gathered. Five components:

  • 1 Substrate taxonomy — exact file list, named owner, update cadence, and what "current" means for each file
  • 2 Signal consolidation plan — which signals need to move from siloed tools into the substrate, and how
  • 3 Intelligence layer spec — the ten most important queries the system should answer, derived from actual friction patterns observed
  • 4 Role mapping — specific names for first DRI assignments and player-coach transitions
  • 5 Pilot spec — exact team, exact outcome, baseline metrics
DAYS 19–20

Validation Sessions

Session 1 — CTO + one senior engineer: Pressure-test the substrate taxonomy and intelligence layer spec. Is the file taxonomy realistic to maintain? Are the intelligence layer queries the right ones?

Session 2 — CEO + Head of Product: Pressure-test the role model and transition plan. Is the DRI model calibrated correctly for their culture? Is the timeline realistic?

DAYS 21–25

Final Deliverable Production

Produce the updated architecture document — the white paper, now specific and actionable — with all five components finalized, validated, and ready to hand to an implementation team.

→ implementation-plan.md → white-paper-v2.html
DAYS 26–30

Stakeholder Presentation + Alignment

Present the final deliverable to CivilGrid leadership. Address remaining questions. Leave with explicit commitment on three things: which team runs the pilot, who the first DRI is and what outcome they own, and when Phase 1 begins.

The engagement does not end with a document handed over. It ends with a named person, a named outcome, and a start date.

— 07

Decision Gates

Three moments where what we find changes the plan. These are not optional checkpoints — they are architectural forks. The right response to each depends entirely on what the evidence shows, and the plan must be willing to change accordingly.

Gate 1 · End of Week 1 Signal Fragmentation Day 5
If signal is fragmented

Phase 1 of the implementation becomes a data consolidation sprint — routing signals from disconnected tools into machine-readable substrate files — before the intelligence layer can be built. The white paper gets a new section on data infrastructure, and the timeline shifts by four to six weeks.

If signal is accessible

Proceed as planned. The intelligence layer can be built immediately on top of existing data, and the timeline compresses. The signal audit workshop output feeds directly into the substrate taxonomy without an intermediate consolidation step.

Gate 2 · End of Week 2 Bottleneck Diagnosis Day 11
If bottleneck is not coordination

If cycle time data shows the primary bottleneck is unclear requirements, technical debt, or hiring gaps rather than coordination overhead, the intelligence layer solves the wrong problem. We address root causes first. An intelligence layer on top of a broken engineering process speeds up the wrong thing.

If bottleneck is coordination

The architecture is correctly targeted. We have quantified the cost of the problem the intelligence layer solves, which becomes the headline metric for the implementation plan's business case.

Gate 3 · End of Week 3 Cultural Readiness Day 18
If readiness is low

The transition plan gets resequenced. A longer pilot with a softer rollout precedes any structural role changes. The DRI model is introduced without explicitly deprecating management titles — the architecture works through demonstrated value, not announced reorganization.

If readiness is high

Accelerate the timeline and consider a more visible structural commitment from leadership as part of the launch. Name the first DRI publicly, retire the first set of status meetings on Day 1 of Phase 1, and treat the pilot as a proof point for a company-wide announcement.
